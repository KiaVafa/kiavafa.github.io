How I make an AI food ad: the Egg Sandwich spot, step by step

By Kia Vafa · 19 November 2026

This is a 17-second concept ad for a breakfast sandwich. Toast, egg and melting cheese drop in and stack into one sandwich. No kitchen, no camera, no food stylist. The picture is generated with AI. Everything around the picture is ordinary film work, and that part is what makes it look like an ad and not like a pile of random clips.

These are the steps I follow.

1. Start with real commercials, not with a prompt

Before I write a single prompt I watch how real food ads are shot. For this one I studied probe-lens commercials: the lens travels one or two centimetres above the table, so a pepper grinder looks like a tower and a sandwich looks like a building. I wrote down three things to copy: the lens stays very low and close, each shot is one smooth move, and slow motion is saved for the one big moment.

2. A small idea and a shot list

An ad needs one idea you can say in a line. Here it is simply: a sandwich builds itself. Then I break it into shots, each two or three seconds long, each with one camera move, with a hook in the first second because that is where people decide to keep watching or scroll.

3. Lock the product

The biggest problem in AI video is that things change from shot to shot. The bun gets seeds, the cheese changes colour, the egg gets a yolk. So before any video I make a product sheet: the same sandwich from the side, from three quarters, and in close-up, on a plain background. Every shot after that uses this sheet as its reference. For a real brand, your own product photos do this job.

4. One look for the whole film

I write one short block of text that describes the light, the lens and the colours, and it goes into every prompt unchanged. Same light direction, same background, same colour of the bun. This is what makes seven separate clips feel like one film.

5. A still frame for every shot

I do not ask the video model to invent a shot from text. I make the first frame of each shot as a still image, and for the difficult ones the last frame too. The video model then only has to move from one picture to the other. It is the difference between giving someone a storyboard and giving them a sentence.

6. Cheap drafts first

Each shot is generated on its own, short, at low resolution. Most first tries are wrong in some way: the cheese melts upward, the bun lands too softly, the logo drifts. I throw those away and only render the good takes at full quality. AI can drift on small text and logos, so the name on the bun is the thing I check hardest.

7. The edit, the grade and the sound

This is where it becomes an ad. I cut the shots on the beat, grade them so the colours match, and add the logo and text in the edit, never in the generation. Then the sound: I'm a music producer, so I write the music for the picture, and every sound effect sits on the thing that causes it, like the whoosh on a move and the thud when the bun lands.

What I need from a brand

For a real product the list is short: a few clear photos of the product from different angles, your logo, one line about who the ad is for, and where it will run. I come back with an idea in a few lines before anything is made.
