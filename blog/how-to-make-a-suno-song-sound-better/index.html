How to make a Suno song sound better: mixing and mastering an AI track

By Kia Vafa · 19 November 2026

A lot of the songs people send me now started in Suno. The writing is often good. The sound almost never is. It is thin, a bit harsh on top, and when you play it next to a released song in a playlist it feels smaller and further away.

Here is why that happens, what you can do about it yourself, and what I do when one of these songs lands on my desk.

Why AI songs sound like demos

An AI music tool does not record a singer, a drummer and a bass player on separate tracks and then mix them. It generates one finished stereo file in one go. That has a few side effects you can hear:

  • A metallic shimmer on top. Cymbals, the breath in a voice and the tail of a reverb often have a swirly, watery sound, a bit like a low-quality MP3.
  • A vocal that is glued to the music. The voice sits inside the instruments instead of in front of them, so the words are harder to follow.
  • A soft low end. Kick and bass blur into one shape instead of being two clear things.
  • Not much width or depth. Everything sits in the middle, at the same distance.
  • It is quieter than released music, or it is loud but flat, with no punch.

What you can do before you export

  1. Download the best file the tool gives you. If you can get a WAV, take the WAV. Every extra round of MP3 compression makes the shimmer worse.
  2. Download the stems if your plan has them. Separate vocal and instrument files give a mixer much more to work with.
  3. Generate a few versions and pick with your ears, not your eyes. Two takes of the same prompt can sound very different. Pick the one with the cleanest vocal, even if another has a slightly better melody. A clean vocal can be mixed. A smeared one stays smeared.
  4. Don't run it through an online "AI mastering" button and call it done. Making a harsh file louder makes it a louder harsh file.

What I do in a mix

When I get an AI song, the work goes in this order.

1. Split it into stems. If I only have one file, I separate it into vocal, drums, bass and the rest of the instruments. Now I can treat the voice without touching the cymbals, and the kick without touching the bass.

2. Clean each part. This is where most of the time goes. I take the harsh, whistling frequencies out of the vocal, calm the shimmer in the top of the instruments, and remove the low rumble that eats headroom. The goal is not to make it dull. The goal is to take away what hurts so I can turn up what is good.

3. Rebuild the balance. The vocal comes forward. Kick and bass each get their own space. I add width and a little depth with reverb and delay that I control, so the song has a front and a back again.

4. Master it. Last, the whole song is brought up to the level of other releases, checked on small speakers, earbuds and a car, and exported in the format the streaming platforms want.

What a mix cannot fix

I would rather tell you this before you pay than after. Some artifacts are baked into the file. If the vocal breaks up into a robotic sound on one word, I can hide it, but I cannot always remove it. If the drums are smeared into the guitars, separation only goes so far. Most songs come out far cleaner, fuller and louder. A few have one or two spots that stay imperfect. I listen first and tell you which kind yours is.
